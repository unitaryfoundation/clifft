\accentO
\begin{frame}{clifft in thirty seconds}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Clifford gates are ``easy'': clifft tracks them \punch{symbolically}, almost for free, however many qubits.

% ================================================================ A: idea
\accentA
\begin{frame}{A \,\textperiodcentered\, The idea: pay for magic, not for dimensions}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item States with \emph{little} magic are secretly simple: they are a \punch{short sum of ``free'' stabilizer states} --- the states Clifford simulators handle natively.
        \item Store $\chi$ small recipes (a few KB each) instead of $2^k$ raw numbers. Like describing an image as a few overlapping patterns instead of listing every pixel.
        \item Clifford gates update all recipes cheaply; only $T$ gates grow their number; \punch{measurements shrink it back}.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85]
        \fill[violet!22] (0,1.9) rectangle (4.6,3.1);
        \draw[violet] (0,1.9) rectangle (4.6,3.1);
        \node[font=\scriptsize,color=ink] at (2.3,2.5) {$2^k$ amplitudes};
        \node[font=\small] at (2.3,1.55) {$=$};
        \foreach \i/\c in {0/1.0,1/0.62,2/0.3}{
          \fill[copper!35] (\i*1.15+0.35,0.15) rectangle (\i*1.15+1.3,1.05);
          \draw[copper] (\i*1.15+0.35,0.15) rectangle (\i*1.15+1.3,1.05);}
        \node[font=\small,color=copper,anchor=west] at (3.78,0.6) {$+\ \cdots$};
        \node[font=\scriptsize,color=mutedink] at (2.3,-0.28) {$\chi$ cheap stabilizer ``recipes''};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{The cost driver becomes the amount of magic $t$, not the dimension $2^k$.}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ A: why it works
\begin{frame}{A \,\textperiodcentered\, Why it should work}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item There is an optimal way to split a $T$ gate so the number of recipes grows only as \punch{$2^{0.228\,t}$} --- far slower than $2^k$ when magic is sparse.
        \item Accepting a small, \punch{tunable} error $\delta$ lets you keep only a random sample of recipes ($\sim 1/\delta^2$ of them). Measured accuracy is \emph{predictable}: error $= 0.50\,\delta$, on the nose.
        \item Everything stays in the language clifft already speaks: same frame idea, same measurement collapse.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.82]
        \draw[->,mutedink] (0,0) -- (5.4,0) node[below left,font=\tiny,color=mutedink]{compiled peak rank $k$ \ (magic-sparse: $t \propto k$)};
        \draw[->,mutedink] (0,0) -- (0,3.4) node[rotate=90,anchor=south east,font=\tiny,color=mutedink,yshift=2pt]{cost (log)};
        \draw[violet,very thick] (0.2,0.15) -- (4.6,3.30) node[anchor=south east,font=\scriptsize,color=violet,pos=0.88,yshift=6pt]{exact $2^{k}$};
        \draw[copper,very thick] (0.2,0.75) -- (5.0,1.90) node[anchor=north west,font=\scriptsize,color=copper,pos=0.72,yshift=-2pt]{recipes $2^{0.228 t}$};
        \fill[ink] (1.46,1.05) circle (1.8pt);

      \end{tikzpicture}

  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{Trade exactness you can measure for reach you cannot otherwise have.}
\end{frame}

  \begin{columns}[T]

% ================================================================ A: generality
\begin{frame}{A \,\textperiodcentered\, It generalizes: any Clifford$+T$ circuit}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The trick for circuits with magic \emph{everywhere}: teleport each in-line $T$ onto its own helper qubit (the same adaptive gadget real hardware uses) --- then all magic sits in \punch{one up-front layer} again.
    \item So the sampler keeps its best properties on \emph{any} circuit: \punch{no penalty for interleaving}, and the normalization stays exactly known --- the expensive estimation step never enters.
    \item Tested at scale on circuits whose answer is known \emph{by construction} (hidden shift: the output is the hidden string, always): at up to \punch{96 total qubits}, the backend returns $0.90$--$0.99$ of the true probability $1$, in seconds.
    \item Honest finding: clifft's compiler crushes many such circuits to tiny cores and answers in microseconds --- which turns ``who wins'' into a \emph{rule}, not a race \,$\rightarrow$\, next slide.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{Generality at the product-magic price --- validated end to end against exact references.}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ B: idea
\accentB
\begin{frame}{B \,\textperiodcentered\, The idea: don't run one shot faster --- run 4{,}096 abreast}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item A clifft gate just \emph{streams} a small array (16 KB--16 MB). One such array cannot keep a GPU busy: \punch{a stadium choir asked to sing one sentence}.
        \item But clifft's workloads are Monte-Carlo: \punch{thousands of shots of the same program}, today run one after another.
        \item Put $B{=}4{,}096$ shots' arrays on the GPU side by side and apply every gate to \emph{all of them} in one launch.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
        % sequential CPU
        \node[font=\scriptsize\bfseries,color=violet,anchor=west] at (0,3.3) {CPU today: single file};
        \foreach \i in {0,1,2,3,4}{\fill[violet!30] (\i*0.62,2.55) rectangle (\i*0.62+0.5,2.95);}
        \draw[->,mutedink] (3.25,2.75) -- (3.9,2.75);
        % batched GPU
        \node[font=\scriptsize\bfseries,color=teal,anchor=west] at (0,1.9) {GPU: all at once};

  \takeaway{Batching doesn't make the GPU faster --- it makes the problem big enough for the GPU to be fast.}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ B: why 1
\begin{frame}{B \,\textperiodcentered\, Why it should work (1): shots march in lockstep}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The shape of a shot --- which operation runs when, and how big the array is at every step --- is \punch{fixed at compile time and identical for every shot}. clifft's compiler already writes this schedule down.
    \item What differs per shot (measurement outcomes, noise) is just \punch{a few numbers}, which enter the shared computation as per-shot parameters --- like SIMD lanes, one lane per shot.
    \item So the classic objection to batching --- ``shots diverge'' --- \emph{does not apply to clifft}. Its structure is unusually batchable.
    \item And nobody serves this niche: the two existing batched-GPU simulators both \punch{fall back to one-shot-at-a-time} exactly when circuits measure mid-flight --- clifft's home turf.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{The hardest architectural question resolves in the design's favor before any GPU code is written.}
\end{frame}

% ================================================================ B: why 2
\begin{frame}{B \,\textperiodcentered\, Why it should work (2): the speed limit is bandwidth --- and GPUs have $7\times$ more}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item These operations do almost no arithmetic per byte: on \emph{any} machine they run at the speed of memory.
        \item Measured: clifft on 11 CPU cores is only $2.6\times$ one core --- \punch{the bus is the limit, not the cores}.
        \item A GH200's memory is $\sim\!7\times$ wider than a server CPU's. Once the batch saturates it: \punch{5--10$\times$ more shots per second}, honestly bounded.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
        \node[font=\scriptsize,color=ink,anchor=west] at (0,2.9) {memory bandwidth (TB/s)};
        \fill[violet!30] (0,1.9) rectangle (0.75,2.3);

% ================================================================ B: risk + test
\begin{frame}{B \,\textperiodcentered\, The one risk, and the \$20 experiment that settles it}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Mid-circuit measurements need a decision from the CPU: results cross to the host, outcomes cross back. Each crossing costs microseconds --- \punch{the same scale as the small kernels themselves}.
    \item Two reasons it should survive: the crossing is paid \punch{once per batch} (nanoseconds per shot at $B{=}4{,}096$), and the GH200's CPU--GPU link is built precisely to make it cheap.
    \item A microbenchmark faithful to clifft's kernels --- \emph{including complete measurements with the round-trip} --- is built, validated, and waiting. GH200 time: \$2/hour, self-serve.
  \end{itemize}
  \centering
  {\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.28\textwidth}p{0.64\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    round-trip measured cheap & \textcolor{teal}{\textbf{go}}: batched backend; first target = replay-style queries (no round-trips at all) \\
    round-trip eats the win & \textbf{no-go}, cleanly --- or on-device sampling is the follow-up \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}
  \takeaway{Either answer is cheap, quantitative, and final --- that is what the microbenchmark is for.}
\end{frame}
