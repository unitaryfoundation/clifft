    Performance¶

    Clifft is built for fast, exact simulation of near-Clifford circuits. Its compiler simplifies the Clifford structure of a circuit ahead of time so each shot only evolves a small active quantum state (with \(2^k\) amplitudes, where \(k\) is the active width) rather than the full qubit register. During sampling, shots that follow the same quantum trajectory share state evolution.

    This page answers three questions: how Clifft compares with other CPU simulators on one and eight cores, how throughput changed in v0.14, and how Clifft's speed has progressed since v0.1.

    CPU simulator throughput¶

    The clifft-bench suite measures attempted shots per second across 18 complete circuits on one and eight physical CPU cores in two common sampling modes:

    • Packed samples (postselection off): return bit-packed detector and observable records for every shot (all 18 circuits).
    • Survivor counts (postselection on): discard shots that trigger postselected detectors and return accepted and logical-error counts (10 postselected circuits).

    Clifft runs with its default compiler and sampling settings (clifft.compile(), batch_size="auto", sharing="auto") and no per-circuit manual tuning. Each cell below counts how many circuits Clifft won out of those measured by both simulators (a dash means no paired measurement):

    On distillation and Reed–Muller circuits, compiler simplification eliminates nearly all non-Clifford work (reducing active width to 0 or 1). On cultivation, fold-transversal, coherent-noise, and Quantum Volume circuits, narrower compiled plans, shared trajectory execution, and multi-core scaling drive Clifft's lead. Where peers are faster on one core—such as xtim on packed cultivation circuits it supports, or Tsim on the fixed-input Draper adder and Cain d=9—Clifft's eight-core scaling narrows the gap or pulls ahead. For an earlier 16-core comparison against dedicated statevector simulators (Qiskit Aer, qsim, and Qulacs) up to QV28, see the Quantum Volume experiment.

    Comparing with Stim on Clifford circuits¶

    On the pure-Clifford Surface code d7, r7 circuit, Clifft and the other compiled samplers outpace Stim on steady-state throughput, which can be misleading without the setup tradeoff. Stim's compile_detector_sampler() propagates a Pauli frame through the circuit's gate layers per batch with minimal setup (~1 ms, single-threaded), whereas Clifft precomputes the exact fault-to-detector and observable maps ahead of time and samples sparse noise directly at active width 0 (and can scale across cores or discard failing shots early in survivor-count mode). Compiled fault-map sampling therefore wins on large shot counts, while Stim avoids compilation overhead on small shot counts or one-off circuits.

    Measurement setup¶

    Rates report median attempted shots per second across three timed repetitions of at least 15 seconds each, measured after compilation and one full warmup call (both reported separately in the widget details and data table). Work deferred until later calls, such as lazy plan construction, remains inside the timed window.

    Each case runs in a fresh worker process pinned to one or eight physical CPU cores on AWS m7a.4xlarge (AMD EPYC 9R14, Ubuntu 24.04) with a 32 GiB memory limit. The comparison covers Clifft 0.14, Tsim 1.0.0, xtim 4.1.2, SymFT 2026.10.8, and Stim 1.16.0; see the Clifft v0.14 results, peer simulator results, review summary, and benchmark contract for exact software commits, environments, and timing boundaries.

    Changes in v0.14¶

    Across all nine paired circuits in survivor-count mode, Clifft v0.14 achieves a 11.1x median speedup on one core (1.74x–65.0x) and 9.83x on eight cores (1.08x–58.2x) over v0.13. Fold-transversal d=5 appears in the peer comparison above; its v0.13 survivor baselines were omitted because of their runtime.

    Three changes drive the v0.14 gains:

    1. Global sampling reconstruction (now on by default) builds narrower sampling plans tailored to the requested outputs—cutting peak active width on d=5 cultivation from 10 to 6—and folds redundant rotations.
    2. Shared trajectory execution (now on by default) avoids repeating quantum state evolution for shots that follow the same trajectory, drops rejected postselected shots early, and reuses worker memory across calls.
    3. Runtime SIMD dispatch and default scheduling: packed CPU kernels automatically use AVX2 or AVX-512 when available, and clifft.compile() now enables active-width scheduling by default.

    Performance over time¶

    The series below tracks the six core QEC circuits present in every benchmark campaign from v0.1 through v0.14. Because Clifft separates ahead-of-time compilation from per-shot sampling, each release can shrink active states or optimize sampling kernels without adding circuit analysis to the sampling loop.

    Four releases drove the largest jumps: v0.8 introduced symbolic sampling plans; v0.10 added compiler width reduction and packed batching; v0.11 added operation scheduling and deferred noise sampling; and v0.14 added global reconstruction and shared trajectory execution, reaching a 134x median chained speedup over v0.1.

    The v0.1 through v0.9 points come from a common history execution, and v0.10 through v0.14 chain each circuit's paired single-core survivor-count current / previous ratio onto that baseline before taking the median across the six circuits (see scripts/render_history.py). Pairing runs both releases on the same host and protocol within each step, though speedup magnitude still reflects the campaign protocol—in particular, the v0.14 campaign moved from m7a.xlarge to m7a.4xlarge, increased call sizes on circuits such as coherent d=5, r=5, and switched Clifft from manual batch calibration and opt-in scheduling to default automatic execution.
