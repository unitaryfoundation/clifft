    Performance¶

    Clifft is built for fast, exact simulation of near-Clifford circuits. Its compiler resolves Clifford coordinates and symbolic dependencies before sampling, then executes only the remaining branch values and dense active-state operations for each shot.

    The measurements below cover three complementary questions: how Clifft compares with other CPU simulators, how its throughput has changed across releases, and how it behaves when a circuit becomes fully dense.

    CPU simulator throughput¶

    The clifft-bench suite measures attempted shots per second for complete circuits on one pinned logical CPU. Each simulator uses its best calibrated batch size for each workload, and Clifft enables opt-in active-width scheduling with standard settings after the default compiler pipeline.

    • Magic-state preparation: Clifft reaches 597 million attempted shots/s on 15-to-1 distillation and 83.6 million on the measured Reed–Muller circuit, where stabilizer recovery and phase simplification collapse the active state. On the injection/cultivation circuits (d=3 and d=5), xtim and Tsim are faster. The separate end-to-end cultivation d=5 workload continues through escape into a distance-15 surface code, with postselection on cultivation-stage detectors, reaching 39.1 thousand shots/s in Clifft and 135 thousand in Tsim.
    • Surface-code memory and coherent noise: Clifft approaches Tsim's throughput on coherent d=3, r=3 (2.78 million vs. 2.89 million shots/s) and leads on coherent d=5, r=5 (12.8 thousand shots/s vs. 3.03 thousand in Tsim and 63.3 in SymFT), where compiled Pauli rotations and bounded active width keep per-shot work small.
    • Arithmetic and code switching: Clifft reaches 199 million shots/s on the fixed-input 16-bit Draper adder (vs. 25.8 million in Tsim) and samples the measured BT27 and BT81 code-switching circuits at 247 thousand and 831 thousand shots/s.
    • Quantum Volume (1 core): Dense circuits drive the active width to the full qubit count. Tsim's active-statevector backend is faster at QV10 (480 thousand vs. 23.2 thousand shots/s in Clifft), whereas at QV20 Clifft's dense statevector execution reaches 5.85 shots/s compared with 0.274 in SymFT and 0.0938 in Tsim.

    Clifford postselection with Stim¶

    On the Clifford Surface code d7, r7 workload in the second panel, all tools sample the same circuit, reject any shot with a detector event, and count logical observable errors among survivors. Clifft reaches 4.22 million attempted shots/s compared with 1.12 million for Stim (3.77x), while xtim and Tsim reach 4.85 million and 5.58 million. Clifft can reject failed shots early and accumulate survivor counts directly. xtim and Tsim also support early rejection, but return records that their adapters reduce to counts. The Stim baseline materializes packed detector and observable records before filtering survivors in the adapter. The comparison includes these output-processing costs.

    Measurement setup¶

    Rates count attempted shots, so postselected shots that are later discarded still count as simulation work. Ahead-of-time compilation is excluded; lazy plan construction during sampling (such as xtim's per-sample plan cache fill) and reduction of packed outputs to aggregate survivor counts are included in the timed window.

    Benchmarks run on AWS m7a.xlarge instances (AMD EPYC 9R14, Ubuntu 24.04) using one pinned logical CPU, one thread, a 12 GiB memory limit, and five timed samples of at least 30 seconds per case. The comparison covers Clifft 0.13, SymFT 0.1.1, xtim 3.2.2, Stim 1.16.0, and Tsim 0.1.5; see the Clifft v0.13 results, peer simulator results, review summary, and benchmark contract for exact software commits, environments, and timing boundaries.

    Changes in v0.13¶

    Across the nine paired workloads, throughput reaches 223x the v0.12 rate on 15-to-1 distillation and 16.2x on Reed–Muller. Peak active width falls from 5 to 0 and 5 to 1, respectively, reducing the dense state carried during sampling. The release's improved stabilizer recovery and phase simplification eliminate more non-Clifford work before sampling. Packed sampling also skips noise-sampling work when a compiled plan has no quantum-noise sites.

    Among the remaining workloads, the fixed-input 16-bit Draper adder improves by 8.21%, coherent d=3, r=3 by 4.74%, and cultivation d=3 by 2.96%. The other four circuits fall within the ±1.1% inconclusive band. Both releases use their default compiler pipeline followed by active-width scheduling and independently calibrated batch sizes.

    Cultivation d5 varies between processes in both releases, so this run does not establish a reliable release-change percentage; see #656.

    Performance over time¶

    While the comparison above measures v0.13 against v0.12 on the expanded benchmark suite, the historical series below tracks a consistent six-workload QEC core from v0.1 through v0.12. Clifft's compiler-and-executor separation allows each release to improve circuit optimization, symbolic planning, executable preparation, or active-state kernels without moving circuit analysis back into the per-shot execution loop.

    The first major step arrived in v0.8, when symbolic plans replaced the original localized-Pauli virtual machine. Version 0.10 combined compiler width reduction with packed sampling—including an 837x gain on coherent d=5, r=5 after reducing peak active width from 24 to 13. Version 0.11 added operation scheduling and deferred noise sampling, and version 0.12 preserved that QEC throughput while adding state-aware compiler simplification, reaching a 13.6x median speedup over v0.1.

    The v0.1 through v0.9 points come from a common history execution, and v0.10 through v0.12 chain paired release ratios onto that baseline as recorded in reporting sources.

    Dense Quantum Volume circuits on 16 cores¶

    Whereas the single-core Quantum Volume rows above compare near-Clifford samplers at QV10 and QV20, this multi-core study compares Clifft against dedicated statevector simulators (Qiskit Aer, qsim, and Qulacs) up to QV28 (\(2^{28}\) amplitudes) where Clifft's near-Clifford representation offers the least help.

    Using 16 physical CPU cores on an AWS c8i.8xlarge (Clifft 0.10), Clifft records the shortest median execution time at QV20 and QV22. At QV28 it completes in 24.7 seconds, compared with 29.9 seconds for Qiskit Aer and 344.9 seconds for Qulacs; qsim leads from QV24 onward and finishes QV28 in 11.4 seconds. Smaller widths favor tools with lower fixed startup overhead. Even outside its intended near-Clifford regime, Clifft remains competitive with dedicated statevector simulators.

    Following the original Clifft paper's protocol, Clifft's timer includes compilation plus one sample, while Qiskit transpilation and Qulacs/qsim circuit preparation occur before their timers. Each width uses three circuit seeds; see the experiment description and complete result table.
